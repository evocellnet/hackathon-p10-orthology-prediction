Background:
Homologous genes are genes that share a common evolutionary origin because they descended from a single ancestral gene. Similarity between gene sequences implies common ancestry. Genes which are related through speciation are called orthologs, as opposed to paralogs, which are related through duplication. Methods such as OrthoFinder or OMA use all-against-all sequence comparisons (implemented in BLAST or Diamond). This computation is O(N^2), which is slow.

Task:
Build an orthology prediction method that is applicable to a scalable number of proteomes (from few dozens to multiple thousands; potentially, up to alphafold database scale) and that reports the inferred relationship of the proteins into hierarchical orthologous groups. The output should include at least a file in the orthoxml format. A successful method would assign all the genes from all given genomes in the correct evolutionary relationships between them, identifying the genes that are all descending from one ancestral gene.

You have complete freedom in the approach to use for the whole task, but you will need to split the problem into smaller, improvable sub-tasks, each with its own benchmark and quality control. For example, in case of a tree-based approach, the tree-building step should be improved and benchmarked by its own before the full pipeline benchmark; similarly, in case of an initial protein family placement step, the protein family placement must be benchmarked itself and optimised, before making it inside the pipeline final benchmark. Another critical point will be the usage or computation of a species tree. Explore the alternatives regarding using a universal species tree, since several protein families might have alternative evolutionary histories that will be difficult to model univocally. 

The method will likely use protein sequence information as this is the predominant form of data that is accessible on online databases for genes. However, test the possibility of including additional data available to support the orthology prediction task. Here you can see a non-complete list of the data you can try to add in any of the sub-steps required to complete the main task. If possible, report the effect of using or not using the additional layers of data in the final benchmark as well as in the sub-task specific benchmark:

Protein 3d structure; the recent scientific development stemming from Alphafold3 and several protein language models resulted in an abundance of predicted protein structure or protein structure representation across the whole tree of life and for several entire genomes. This information could be beneficial for remote homology (foldseek), tree building (foldtree), and thus, eventually, orthology. Try to integrate protein 3d structure in orthology prediction in any way it seems fit. One of the initial attempts could involve the 3di representation of protein structure, because it neatly corresponds to protein sequence, is easily obtainable at scale, and has been integrated in several existing methods.

Synteny; the genomic context tends to be maintained over some evolutionary times. So far this has not been exploited very much in orthology prediction methods, but rather in some post-processing steps as verifications. There could be potential to integrate this information in the predicting part of orthology as well. For most reference genomes, the .gff file containing gene location is obtainable along the genome and proteome from the reference database.

Genomic context; multiple additional layers of data exists, mostly from OMICs experiments on model species, that could be explored as a way to detect conservation, for example tracking the corresponding copy of paralogous genes using gene expression, epigenetics, etc. Explore the possibility of including protein expression, chip seq or methylation experiments, or other ancillary annotations that could help the orthology inference. Keep track and report the conservation and conservation breaks of this information. This data is not trivial to obtain at a large scale, therefore feel free to discard this idea if it is not feasible for implementing in the overall task. Isoforms; When available, you could use isoform information as an additional layer of information. Alternative splicing might only be useful at certain taxonomic ranges and only a few representative lineages. However, it might contain interesting information in addition to establish orthology, or even orthologous isoforms.

Genome (DNA) sequence; it might be re-used to validate the effective loss of a gene. For example, when an orthologous group is not found in specific taxa, the genomic locus of neighbouring orthologous genes might be investigated to validate the effective loss, by observing changes in local synteny or pseudogenization. This might give additional data to validate gene presence/absence and the placement of the gene orthologous group in the correct taxonomic level.
